Why do prices end in .99?

Nine ninety-nine is basically ten. So why does it feel like nine?

We read prices left to right. The first digit lands first, and it sticks. So nine ninety-nine feels like nine, not ten. That's the left-digit effect. One cent off, and the price feels a whole dollar cheaper. It works when the left digit changes, like $2.99 against $3.00.

A clothing catalogue tested a 9 ending. A dress sold about a third more at $39 than at $34. At $44, it sold no better than at $34. The same researchers found that a $9 ending raised sales in all three of their field tests.

So a 9 at the end isn't an accident. Next time you see one, read the whole price.

Receipts

Where the facts in this video come from.

  1. Left-digit effect a 9-ending price feels smaller than a price one cent higher, but only when the left digit changes ($2.99 vs $3.00), and more when the prices are compared side by side. Thomas & Morwitz, "Penny Wise and Pound Foolish: The Left-Digit Effect in Price Cognition", Journal of Consumer Research 32(1), 2005, pp. 54–64. Sources: ideas.repec.org, sciencedaily.com
  2. Dress test, $34 → $39 = demand up about a third; $44 = no different from $34 Anderson & Simester, "Mind Your Pricing Cues", Harvard Business Review, Sept 2003, pp. 96–103. Sources: kellogg.northwestern.edu, dish.andrewsullivan.com, chicagobooth.edu
