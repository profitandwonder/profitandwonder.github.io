Why does the medium popcorn exist?

That medium popcorn exists to sell you the large.

Picture two sizes. Small, seven dollars. Large, ten. Ten feels like a lot, so most people pick small. Now add a medium for nine fifty. Suddenly the large is only fifty cents more. What a deal. Large it is.

That's the decoy effect. An option nobody wants makes another one look like a bargain. Researchers first described it in 1982.

Dan Ariely tested it with an Economist subscription offer and 100 MIT students. Without the decoy, 32 percent chose the print-and-web bundle. With the decoy added, 84 percent did. The effect is weaker with real products than in number tables, but it shows up again and again.

Next time you see three prices, ask: which one is the decoy?

Receipts

Where the facts in this video come from.

  1. Popcorn prices ($7 / $9.50 / $10) are an illustration, not data. They sit inside 2026 US cinema price ranges (AMC/Regal small ~$7–8.60, medium ~$8–9.60, large ~$9.50–10.60, from price trackers, not official lists): Sources: cinemasnackprices.com, cinemafoodprice.com
  2. Decoy effect (asymmetric dominance) adding an option that is clearly worse than one option (but not the other) raises the share of the better one. Huber, Payne & Puto, "Adding Asymmetrically Dominated Alternatives: Violations of Regularity and the Similarity Hypothesis", Journal of Consumer Research 9(1), 1982. doi:10.1086/208899. Replicated many times, but the effect is weaker with real products and pictures than with number tables (Frederick, Lee & Baskin, "The Limits of Attraction", J. Marketing Research, 2014).
  3. Economist test, 84% vs 32% Ariely, Predictably Irrational (2008), ch. 1. Offer: web $59, print $125, print + web $125. With all three: 16 / 0 / 84. Without print-only: 68 web / 32 bundle. Sample: 100 MIT Sloan students, Ariely's own experiment (not The Economist's sales data). Sources: en.wikipedia.org, sketchplanations.com
