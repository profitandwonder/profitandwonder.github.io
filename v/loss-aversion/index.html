Why does losing hurt more than winning?

Losing a hundred dollars hurts more than winning a hundred feels good.

Picture a coin flip. Win a hundred and fifty, or lose a hundred. Most people say no, even though the win is bigger. Why? Your brain weighs a loss about twice as much as the same gain. It's called loss aversion. Kahneman and Tversky described it in 1979.

And it holds up. In 2024, researchers pooled 607 estimates from 150 articles. On average, losses weigh about two to one. Some researchers argue the effect is weaker for small stakes, so "about" matters.

That's why ads say "don't miss out" and "last chance". They show you a loss.

So if you're selling something, show people what they'll lose, not just what they'll get.

Receipts

Where the facts in this video come from.

  1. Loss aversion, value curve steeper for losses Kahneman & Tversky, "Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk", Econometrica (1979). Tversky & Kahneman (1992) estimated a loss aversion coefficient of 2.25.
  2. "About two to one" on average Brown, Imai, Vieider & Camerer, "Meta-Analysis of Empirical Estimates of Loss Aversion", Journal of Economic Literature 62(2), 2024. 607 estimates from 150 articles. Mean λ = 1.955 (95% interval 1.82–2.10); unadjusted median 1.69. Sources: aeaweb.org, authors.library.caltech.edu
  3. The coin flip ($150 vs $100) Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011), ch. 26: "For most people, the fear of losing $100 is more intense than the hope of gaining $150."
  4. Some researchers (Gal & Rucker, 2018) argue loss aversion is weaker or absent for small stakes. Gal & Rucker, 2018
