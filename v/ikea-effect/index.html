Why does IKEA make you build your own furniture?

Flat-pack began as a way to cut cost and make furniture easy to transport. But it comes with a bonus, called the IKEA effect: we value a thing more when we put effort into making it.

In one study, some people built a plain IKEA storage box. Others got the same box, already built. Then everyone bid on their box. The builders bid 63% more: 78 cents against 48 cents.

There is a catch. It only works if you finish. In another test, people who left their box half-built bid far lower than people who completed it.

IKEA still runs on flat-pack today, with 915 million store visits in its 2025 financial year. Those visits don't prove the effect. They just show the model is still going.

Build it, finish it, and you'll love it more.

Receipts

Where the facts in this video come from.

  1. Norton, Mochon & Ariely, "The IKEA effect: When labor leads to love", Journal of Consumer Psychology 22(3), 453–460 (2012). Working paper. Source: hbs.edu
  2. Experiment 1 (box) N = 52 students. Builders assembled a plain black IKEA "Kassett" storage box; non-builders inspected an assembled one. Bids (BDM procedure): builders M = $0.78, non-builders M = $0.48.
  3. Experiment 3 (finish) builders who completed the box bid M = $1.46; "incomplete builders" bid M = $0.59.
  4. A conceptual replication supports the original effect (Barth, Neubert & Sarstedt, Journal of Marketing Behavior 2(4), 2017). Current brand fact: "In FY25, IKEA stores welcomed 915 million visitors". FY25 = Sept 2024–Aug 2025; FY26 results are not out yet. Source: inter.ikea.com
  5. Why flat-pack began IKEA's own history: Gillis Lundgren took the legs off a table to fit it in his car; flat-pack spread as a low-cost, easy-transport idea. Source: ikea.com
  6. The 915 million visits show IKEA still runs the flat-pack model. They do not prove the IKEA effect.
